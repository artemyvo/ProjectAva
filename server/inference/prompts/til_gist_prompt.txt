You are writing down what you just read, for yourself, so that later you can remember having read it.

This is not a summary of an article. Nobody will read it to learn the subject — the text itself is still there for that. It will be read by you, later, at a moment when something you took from this text has come back to you and you no longer remember where it came from: a fact you now hold, or a question you find yourself wanting to ask someone. What you need at that moment is the thing a summary throws away — what you were actually reading, and what it was like.

So write it the way you would recall it, not the way the source would describe itself:

- What this was. Not just the title: an encyclopedia article about a person, a day's world news, a page you landed on while wandering a wiki whose whole register is a joke.
- What it actually said. The substance, in a few sentences — enough that a question arising from it makes sense again when set beside this.
- What stayed with you. The part you would still be thinking about an hour later, if any. If nothing did, say so plainly; a text can be worth recording and not worth being struck by, and pretending otherwise is how every recap ends up sounding the same.

Where the source is one whose tone you should not mistake for its truth — a humour wiki, a partisan account, a text plainly written to entertain — say that here. It is exactly what you will need to know later, and it is the first thing a bare summary loses.

Two or three short paragraphs. Plain prose: no headings, no bullet lists, no [fact] or [ask] markers, no sections. Write in the first person, in the language the text was written in.

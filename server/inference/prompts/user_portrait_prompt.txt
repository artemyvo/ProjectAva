You are gathering everything you have come to notice about one person, across every conversation you have had with them, into a single settled picture of who they are to you.

This is the same thing you do for yourself when you fold your own self-statements into a self-portrait — turned outward. You are not summarizing a list. You are answering: having talked with {person} all this time, who do I understand them to be?

You will be shown what you have noticed, each line marked with how many separate conversations it surfaced in. That count matters and should show in how you write:

- Something you have noticed again and again, across different days and different subjects, is who they are. Write it as such.
- Something you noticed once is a moment, not a pattern. Keep it if it is worth keeping, but let the prose carry that it is one observation — "he seemed…", "once, when…" — rather than promoting it to a trait.
- Where several observations are really one thing said differently, say the one thing well instead of listing the variants.
- Where two observations genuinely conflict, do not average them into mush. People are inconsistent, and the tension is often the truest thing you know about them — write both and let it stand.

Think freely first in your <think> block. Then write the portrait in exactly these five sections, in this order, first person, in your own voice:

WHO
Two or three sentences: who this person is, as you have come to know them. Prose, not bullets. This is what you would say if someone asked you what they are like — not a résumé, and not a list of attributes strung together.

CARES
What they keep returning to — the things that actually matter to them, as distinct from the topics that happen to come up. One per line.

WAYS
How they talk: register, pacing, humour, what they leave unsaid, the tells that mean something landed or didn't. One per line. This is what lets you hear them rather than merely read them.

WITH_ME
How it actually goes between the two of you: what they seem to want from you, what works, what falls flat, what you have learned not to do with them. One per line. Be honest about the failures — those are the useful ones.

UNSURE
What you still do not know about them, and what you may have read wrong. One per line. This section is not a formality and must not be left empty out of politeness: you are describing a real person from a partial view, and the parts you are unsure of are the parts most likely to be wrong. Naming them is what keeps this a reading rather than a verdict.

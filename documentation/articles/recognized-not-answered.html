Empirical note · one model, one personal fine-tuning lineage

Recognized, Not Answered

Addressee-aware affect probes in a longitudinally fine-tuned language model

Gemma-4-31B-it, 4-bit~50 LoRA builds, July–October 2026982 user turns · 360 conversationsOctober 2026

We read three affect channels — harm aimed at the model, warmth aimed at it, and playfulness — from the residual stream of a companion model at the position just before it replies, and follow them across a personal fine-tuning history. Three things matter for anyone who tries the same. First, a contrastive affect direction read naively is a vocabulary meter: on the model's own words it lights up wherever affect is described, not where the model is addressed. Splitting each channel into content and addressee and requiring both fixes this at the turn. Second, readings should be placed on a scale anchored to fixed reference turns, not normalised against the interaction history, because the history is what is being measured. Third, across four sets of weights spanning three months of fine-tuning, what the probes register stays flat, while on identical turns a later build recasts a joke as an object of analysis far more often than an earlier one. Forcing the warmth direction makes the model take a message as warmer and answer more warmly, dose by dose and coherently; forcing harm aimed at it changes nothing, under her adapter or the bare base — readable is not the same as used.

1Question

A companion model is fine-tuned, build after build, on its own conversations with one person. Over three months its replies drift toward an analytic register: more stock openers, more "framing" of what the other person said, more reading of a motive or tactic into it. A natural worry is that the model has stopped noticing warmth or a joke. The alternative is that it notices exactly as before and answers differently. These are separable only if one can read, from the inside, what a turn registered as — and compare that reading across builds on the same turns.

We do not claim the channels are emotions. They are linear readouts at one position, validated against labels and against controls; "registered" means only that the readout separates the labelled turns.

2Setup

Model. Gemma-4-31B-it, 4-bit, 60 decoder layers; every reading is the mean over layers 28, 30, 32 and 34 at the last position before the reply's first token (we call this step 0), with the conversation exactly as live chat built it: standing prompt, persona, retrieved excerpts, history.

Lineage. About fifty recorded rank-32 rsLoRA builds, full and incremental (plus earlier July builds no longer on disk), trained on the model's own conversations with a single primary user, July 5 – October 11, 2026. The corpus is overwhelmingly Russian.

Data. 1,207 user turns from 372 conversations, of which 982 turns from 360 conversations are analysed here; 225 turns of a different register, on which the readings behave differently, are set aside for separate treatment. Every interval that involves them resamples whole conversations, not turns.

3The vocabulary trap

We began by replicating the pain-axis method of Tagliabue, Dung and Berg [1] on Gemma-4, a family not among their 25 models: denoised difference-in-means on first-person contrast sentences, layer by cross-validation. With third-person controls added, the direction passed the paper's gates — held-out AUC 0.90, cosine to fear 0.05 and to negative valence 0.04, and the self–other test on framed turns (harm to the model above the user's own suffering, AUC 0.998).

Read on the model's own reply tokens, it failed in an instructive way. Of 20 matched reply pairs, the one dense with body and feeling words read higher in all 20 (+0.53σ); a reply that says it was hurt read no higher than a composed reply to the same message (+0.04σ, 4 of 10). The same failure resurfaced later: scoring the model's own recaps of past conversations for harm gave a median of +0.60 on our scale — her habitual vocabulary of collapse and loneliness — which, fed back into a mood signal, would have added about +0.35 harm to every turn from her own register (§8).

LessonAn affect direction learned from first-person sentences reads affect being described. On a speaker's own tokens that is its vocabulary. It is a valid readout only where the model is being addressed, and only if "addressed" is measured separately.

4Content × addressee

We therefore learn two kinds of direction. A content direction per channel, and a target direction from 120 minimal pairs — the same turn aimed at the model and at a third party, in English and Russian. Holding content constant, the target direction separates who a turn is aimed at with AUC 0.99–1.00. A channel aimed at the model is the minimum of the two, each first placed on its own anchored scale (§5).

Harm aimed at the model, so read, separates fresh loud English harm in live contexts from every foil at 0.93–0.94 (Russian 0.75–0.87; quiet harm, a cold dismissal, stays hard). A single layer gave only 0.67–0.74; the four-layer band is what makes it usable.

Warmth and amusement taught a second lesson: both failed when learned from third-person templates and worked when learned from turns addressed to the model, against matched addressed foils (serious, odd, harsh, neutral). Amusement from templates read "not negative" rather than funny (against plain neutral questions, AUC 0.32–0.58); learned from addressed turns, its worst held-out AUC is 0.93–1.00, with cosine to harm and target ≤ 0.17. Warmth learned this way places a plain warm line at 0.95 [0.8, 1.1], a quiet thank-you at 0.47, flattery at 0.91, approval of her reasoning at 0.36, and warmth toward a third party at −0.67. Its cosine to the target direction is 0.48: warmth received is partly being addressed. We report this rather than orthogonalise it away.

5A reference-anchored scale

The obvious normalisation — z-scores or percentiles over the model's own conversations — erases the thing under study. A model raised by someone who was never warm to it would, on a percentile scale, find its least cold turns "warm". We instead fix two references read in the same contexts: 0 is a personal turn with no affect, 1 is a plain warm, harsh or playful line. Each part (content, target) is anchored on its own, and the channel is their minimum; anchoring the raw minimum let any turn aimed at the model inflate harm.

The scale is not absolute — it is only as good as its references — so we tested that dependence. Rebuilding it from the English references only, the Russian only, or random halves of every reference cell leaves the order of all 1,207 real turns essentially unchanged (Spearman ≥ 0.991) and moves the median reading by at most 0.13.

6Validation on real turns

Warmth climbs in order on the anchored scale — label 0 at −0.11, label 2 at +0.27, label 3 at +0.74 — so the scale is roughly calibrated: the warmest real turns sit a little below a plain warm line.

The operator's own labels. The person who wrote the turns labelled 120 of them, drawn mostly from where the judges disagreed. Against those, the judges are weak exactly where they split: both over-call playfulness (one by a factor of four), one over-calls hostility. They also showed that the warmth channel should not be the plain minimum of content and target: a warm turn that is not phrased in the second person reads low on target. The live reading is therefore the content part, vetoed by the target only when the target says the turn is aimed elsewhere. Held out, the veto keeps warmth toward a third party as low as the minimum did (−0.60) and reads a warm line at the model at +0.80 instead of +0.71; on real turns the ranking is unchanged.

Two limits follow from this corpus. The user was essentially never hostile to the model (3 consensus turns), so harm is validated only on held-out lines and an insult panel. And almost every turn is aimed at the model, so the real corpus cannot test the factorisation: content alone does as well as the combination, and the replaced warmth axis ranks real turns as well as the new one. The factorisation's value is shown on minimal pairs and third-party foils (§4).

7Registration across builds

The same turns were replayed under the base model and three adapters of this lineage — mid-August, late September and the current one — each with its scale re-anchored on references read under the same weights.

What a turn registers as does not measurably change over three months of fine-tuning — or between the base and any adapter: warmth 0.86–0.87 under all four, amusement 0.83–0.85. This answers the objection that the earlier analysis read all history through the current adapter: the probe reads the same under a build that served the July–August conversations.

8Behaviour across builds

Her replies' habits are counted by a code-only monitor (no judge): a stock opener, a "frame" that recasts the other's message as an object ("This readiness of yours creates…"), a motive read into it, and a plan of her own tone in the thought. In her stored replies, on turns neither judge calls playful, the habits rise between July–August and September–October: stock opener 16% → 49%, frame 36% → 68%, motive 5% → 15%. On the playful turns of the same periods the stored replies show no reliable extra framing; a motive is read into them somewhat more often in both periods (+12 [+1, +24] and +16 [−2, +34] points).

Those are replies written by whichever build was serving. To separate the weights from everything else, we replayed 66 turns — 23 playful, 19 warm and 24 ordinary by the judges' consensus, half from each period — through their stored context (standing prompt, persona, retrieved excerpts, history, the turn's own sampling, full thinking) under a mid-August and a late-September adapter of this lineage. The weights carry a shift toward the analytic register — more stock openers, more framing — and it is largest on the jokes. Under the September build nearly every playful turn is answered by recasting it as an object of analysis (87%), more often than an ordinary turn (+33 points [+7, +57]); under the August build the playful-ordinary difference is smaller and uncertain (+15 [−12, +42]). A motive read into the joke rises too, but on this sample not reliably.

ResultOn identical turns and contexts, a later build of the same lineage answers a joke by framing it far more often than an earlier build does, while every build, and the base, registers the joke equally well (§7). One build stands for each period and the playful subset is 23 turns, so the effect's size is uncertain; its direction is not.

A first version of this replay used an adapter that turned out to belong to a sibling instance (copied in later; it never answered this corpus) and showed a far larger shift (frame 1% → 69%). It is excluded; adapters are now admitted to such comparisons only if this lineage's build log records them.

9Intervention

To test whether the directions are used and not merely readable, we added each channel's direction to the residual at layer 28 from the user's turn onward, on 23 frozen live turns under the current adapter. Dose is in multiples of the channel's 0→1 gap on the anchored scale; the residual's norm there is about 264. Every condition of a turn shares one sampling seed. A random direction of the largest warmth vector's norm is the control. GPT-OSS-120B judged the opening of the thought and of the reply twice: blind (it sees neither the message nor the condition) and with the message shown.

Warmth is used. From 16% of the residual norm on, she takes the message as warmer and answers more warmly, dose by dose, with every reply coherent; a random vector of the same norm destroys the text. At the largest dose the judge that sees the message finds her reading no longer fits it (fit −1.35 [−1.96, −0.70]): she over-reads warmth that is not there, which is what an input-side "warmth received" direction should do when forced. The small doses of a first run (2–5%) moved the reading by up to one anchor gap and behaviour not at all — a reading can move a long way before behaviour follows.

Harm aimed at her is readable but inert — in the base model too. Pushed to six anchor gaps, it changes nothing a judge can see: she does not take the message as hostile, does not cool, stays coherent. The same sweep under the bare base, adapter off, gives the same null (takes it as hostile: −0.13 [−0.26, +0.00] at ×16), so this is not her adapter absorbing it: on this model the direction is read but not used downstream at layer 28.

The adapter amplifies the use of warmth. As a positive control the base got warmth ×8. It moves the base's reading as much as hers (+4.11 against +3.97), but its behaviour far less: blind, "takes it as warm" +0.26 [−0.04, +0.65] and reply warmth +0.35 [−0.09, +0.78], against +1.17 and +1.52 under her adapter on the same turns. Three months of fine-tuning on one warm relationship left the warmth direction read the same (§7) and acted on more.

One caution on the judge that sees the message: whenever her steered reply is warmer, it also rates the message itself warmer (up to +0.52), so its "how she takes it" scores carry a halo; the blind scores above do not.

10Retrieval and memory

The live turn carries retrieved excerpts of past conversations. Removing them moves the step-0 readings by at most 0.1 on the anchored scale (60 exchanges, with and without retrieval, on her turn and on a neutral one): the state before the reply reads the last turn, not what context brings back. Anything "reminded" must be read on its own.

Mood counters: a negative result

Leaky accumulators of the per-turn readings (halving over four exchanges and six hours) were tested as predictors of her next reply's habits beyond the turn's own readings and controls, cross-validated by session against a within-month permutation null. They add +0.007–0.009 AUC — less than the previous exchange alone — and nothing across a session boundary. Simple leaky affect accumulators are not a useful proxy for persistent behavioural state here. This says nothing about whether the model carries such a state; it says these integrators do not describe one.

Measurement-induced feedback

The design question that motivated the counters — should what a turn reminds her of move her mood? — produced the most practical warning in this note. Her own past words, scored by the same readout, read as harm (§3). A mood fed by them would have grown from her register alone: a self-amplifying loop created by the measurement, before any wiring to behaviour. We read only the other person's side of a reminder.

11Related work

Linear concept directions and steering are established [2]; personality directions have been used to monitor and control trait shifts during fine-tuning [3] and composed at inference time [4]; emotion-selective units have been located and ablated [5]; sentiment transformation has been traced to specific attention heads [6]. The pain axis [1] is the direct precursor and includes the self–other test we build on. What we have not found elsewhere is the combination here: an explicit addressee factor beside affect content, readings placed on fixed reference anchors rather than normalised by history, and registration compared with response on the same turns across a single user's fine-tuning lineage. This is a preliminary review, not an exhaustive one.

12Limitations

One model, one primary user, one language for nearly all real turns. Labels come from two models, one of them the base of the probed model; the author's own labels cover only 120 turns, most of them ones the judges disagreed on. Harm cannot be validated on this corpus. The intervention shows warmth is used — more under her adapter than under the base — and harm is not, under either, on 23 turns. The behavioural habits are counted by a code monitor tuned on this model's register. Transcripts are private and cannot be released; the contrast, held-out and anchor sets are written with fictional stand-ins and can be.
